Cape Canaveral, Florida. A Titan rocket hurls a robot the size of a small car toward the outer planets.

1979 · Jupiter
On the moon Io, Voyager spots erupting volcanoes — the first ever seen beyond Earth.
1980 · Saturn
A slingshot past the moon Titan flings Voyager up and out of the plane of the planets. It will never visit another world.

Years pass. The Sun shrinks to a bright star. There is nothing left to photograph.
Mission complete. The plan: switch the cameras off to save power.
But astronomer Carl Sagan, part of Voyager's imaging team, has one more request:
Turn the camera around. Take one picture of home.
@panelsfromspace · Ep 014/8 · Swipe >>
The catch: seen from out there, Earth sits right beside the Sun.
Aim the cameras that way and the glare could burn them out.
For years the answer is "not yet". Voyager keeps falling away from the Sun at 17 km every second.
1989
Approval finally comes — from the head of NASA.

February 14, 1990
6 billion km from home. Voyager turns.
It shoots 60 frames: a family portrait of the solar system, from outside looking in.
Out here, even sunlight needs five and a half hours to arrive.
@panelsfromspace · Ep 016/8 · Swipe >>
In one frame, caught in a ray of scattered sunlight...
Earth.
...a speck. Smaller than a single pixel.

"That's here.
That's home.
That's us."
— Carl Sagan
34 minutes later the cameras were shut down for good.
Voyager 1 is still flying — the most distant thing humans have ever made.
